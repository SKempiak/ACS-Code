\section{Block-Size Sweep}\label{sec:block-size}
\rubric{Block-size and pattern sweeps}{35}{Complete matrix and clear interpretation of IOPS and MiB/s
against latency.}

\begin{hypothesis}
  As the block grows from \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} to \qty{256}{\kibi\byte} at QD=1, small-block I/O should be
  \emph{IOPS-limited} (throughput $=$ IOPS $\times$ block, rising with block size) and large-block I/O
  should approach a \emph{throughput ceiling} where IOPS falls as $1/\text{block}$. Sequential should beat
  random at every block size, with the gap largest at small blocks where per-op overhead dominates.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{knobs}
  Fixed       & one job; \code{O_DIRECT}; 5 repetitions of \qty{4}{\second} for reads; 3 repetitions
                capped at \qty{256}{\mebi\byte} each for writes \\
  Varied      & block size \qty{4}{\kibi\byte}--\qty{1}{\mebi\byte} (8 sizes); random vs.\ sequential;
                100\% read vs.\ 100\% write; QD=1 (all four combinations) and QD=32 (sequential read and
                write) \\
  Ceiling     & PCIe~3.0~$\times$4: \qty{3756}{\mebi\byte\per\second} raw (\cref{sec:method}) \\
  Script      & \code{SSD_Performance_Profiling/scripts/run.sh block_size} \\
\end{knobs}

\subsection{Analysis}
\textbf{Random reads at QD=1 are latency-bound, not size-bound.} IOPS fall only from
\csvq{block_size}{bs=4096,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{iops_median} at \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} to
\csvq{block_size}{bs=262144,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{iops_median} at \qty{256}{\kibi\byte}, so
throughput climbs from \csvq{block_size}{bs=4096,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{mibps_median} to
\csvq{block_size}{bs=262144,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s. Per-request latency is
not fixed: it grows from
\csvq[0]{block_size}{bs=4096,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{lat_mean_us_median} to
\csvq[0]{block_size}{bs=262144,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{lat_mean_us_median}\,\si{\micro\second}
(\csvget\ssdA{block_size}{bs=262144,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{lat_mean_us_median}%
\csvget\ssdB{block_size}{bs=4096,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{lat_mean_us_median}%
\ssdratio{\ssdA}{\ssdB}$\times$) over a $64\times$ range of sizes. A useful decomposition is
$W(b)\approx W_0 + t_\text{xfer}(b)$: a fixed access cost $W_0$ of roughly \qty{50}{\micro\second}
(NAND sense plus command overhead, visible at \qty{4}{\kibi\byte}) plus a transfer term. Up to
\qty{256}{\kibi\byte} the transfer term grows \emph{sub-linearly} with $b$, because the controller
stripes a request across several dies and planes and reads them in parallel. Beyond that the stripes
are exhausted. Latency then doubles with size
(\csvq[0]{block_size}{bs=524288,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{lat_mean_us_median}\,\si{\micro\second} at
\qty{512}{\kibi\byte}, \csvq[0]{block_size}{bs=1048576,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{lat_mean_us_median}\,\si{\micro\second}
at \qty{1}{\mebi\byte}), and throughput plateaus at
\csvq{block_size}{bs=524288,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{mibps_median}--%
\csvq{block_size}{bs=1048576,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s.

\textbf{Sequential reads at QD=1 hit a single-request limit.} The \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} sequential read
takes only \csvq[1]{block_size}{bs=4096,pattern=seq,mode=read,qd=1}{lat_mean_us_median}\,\si{\micro\second},
versus \csvq[1]{block_size}{bs=4096,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{lat_mean_us_median} for random.
The controller detects the stream and reads ahead, so most requests hit its internal buffer. From
\qty{64}{\kibi\byte} to \qty{256}{\kibi\byte}, throughput is flat at
\csvq{block_size}{bs=65536,pattern=seq,mode=read,qd=1}{mibps_median}--%
\csvq{block_size}{bs=131072,pattern=seq,mode=read,qd=1}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s, and IOPS fall as $1/b$.
Larger requests then lift it to
\csvq{block_size}{bs=1048576,pattern=seq,mode=read,qd=1}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s at \qty{1}{\mebi\byte},
because each command covers more of the internal pipeline. Even that is only
\qty{48}{\percent} of the \qty{3756}{\mebi\byte\per\second} PCIe~3.0~$\times$4 link. The QD=1 limit is
therefore how fast the controller streams one command at a time, not the interface. The
sequential/random gap is widest at \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} and narrows as both patterns become
transfer-dominated.

\textbf{Deep queues do not reach the link ceiling} (\cref{fig:block-size-qd}). At QD=32, sequential
reads flatten at \csvq{block_size}{bs=16384,pattern=seq,mode=read,qd=32}{mibps_median}--%
\csvq{block_size}{bs=65536,pattern=seq,mode=read,qd=32}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s from \qty{16}{\kibi\byte}
upward. They reach \csvq{block_size}{bs=1048576,pattern=seq,mode=read,qd=32}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s at
\qty{1}{\mebi\byte}, about \qty{56}{\percent} of the raw link rate and well below the
\qtyrange{85}{90}{\percent} that TLP/DLLP overhead would allow. The binding limit is the drive's
NAND/controller back end, not PCIe. Sequential writes at QD=32 are flat at
\csvq{block_size}{bs=65536,pattern=seq,mode=write,qd=32}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s for every block from
\qty{16}{\kibi\byte} up. That is the SLC-cache ingest rate: the same \ensuremath{\sim}\qty{1.06}{\gibi\byte\per\second}
appears in the preconditioning passes and in the first seconds of the sustained fill
(\cref{sec:device-state}).

\csvget\ssdHasRR{block_size}{pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=32}{mibps_median}%
\ifx\ssdHasRR\empty\else
\textbf{Random reads at QD=32} reach
\csvq{block_size}{bs=4096,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=32}{iops_median} IOPS at \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} and
\csvq{block_size}{bs=1048576,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=32}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s at \qty{1}{\mebi\byte}
(dash-dotted curve in \cref{fig:block-size-qd}), against
\csvq{block_size}{bs=1048576,pattern=seq,mode=read,qd=32}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s for sequential reads.
\fi

\textbf{Writes at QD=1.} At \qty{4}{\kibi\byte}, random writes post
\csvq{block_size}{bs=4096,pattern=rand,mode=write,qd=1}{iops_median} IOPS against
\csvq{block_size}{bs=4096,pattern=rand,mode=read,qd=1}{iops_median} for reads. The write buffer
acknowledges before the NAND program, so write latency reflects the host$\to$buffer transfer, not flash
timing. Accordingly, the random and sequential write curves coincide up to \qty{128}{\kibi\byte}
(\csvq{block_size}{bs=65536,pattern=rand,mode=write,qd=1}{mibps_median} vs.\
\csvq{block_size}{bs=65536,pattern=seq,mode=write,qd=1}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s at \qty{64}{\kibi\byte}).
Both flatten at about \qty{1.05}{\gibi\byte\per\second} from \qty{64}{\kibi\byte}, the same ingest rate
as at QD=32. Unlike reads, writes reach their ceiling with a single request in flight.

\textbf{An SLC-exhaustion episode inside the sweep.} The random-write points at
\qty{256}{\kibi\byte}, \qty{512}{\kibi\byte} and \qty{1}{\mebi\byte} fell to
\csvq{block_size}{bs=262144,pattern=rand,mode=write,qd=1}{mibps_median}--%
\csvq{block_size}{bs=1048576,pattern=rand,mode=write,qd=1}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s, with p99 latencies of
\qtyrange{33}{45}{\milli\second}. That is the same \ensuremath{\sim}\qty{60}{\mebi\byte\per\second}
direct-to-QLC rate the sustained experiment measures once the SLC cache is full. These were the last
write points before the sequential-read group, and the run had written about \qty{10}{\gibi\byte} by
then (preconditioning, \code{zero_queue} and the smaller write points). The sequential writes, measured
a few minutes later after the read-only group, were back at \ensuremath{\sim}\qty{1.05}{\gibi\byte\per\second}.
This is consistent with the controller having drained part of the cache in between. Treat the three
collapsed points as a drive-state measurement, not a block-size effect: the sequential-write curve
shows no such drop at the same sizes.
