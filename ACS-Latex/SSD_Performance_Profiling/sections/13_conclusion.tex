\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The Micron 2210 NVMe SSD shows the queueing-driven throughput--latency tradeoff the project set out to
measure. At QD=1, \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} random reads cost
\csvq[1]{zero_queue}{mode=read,pattern=rand}{lat_mean_us_median}\,\si{\micro\second} and writes only
\csvq[1]{zero_queue}{mode=write,pattern=rand}{lat_mean_us_median}\,\si{\micro\second}, because the
controller's write buffer hides the flash program.

Block size moves the bottleneck from per-request latency to bandwidth. With one request in flight,
sequential reads stay near \qty{1.4}{\gibi\byte\per\second} from 64 to \qty{256}{\kibi\byte} and reach
\csvq{block_size}{bs=1048576,pattern=seq,mode=read,qd=1}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s at \qty{1}{\mebi\byte}. At
QD=32 they top out at \csvq{block_size}{bs=65536,pattern=seq,mode=read,qd=32}{mibps_median}\,MiB/s,
\qty{57}{\percent} of the \qty{3756}{\mebi\byte\per\second} PCIe~3.0~$\times$4 link. The NAND back end,
not the interface, is the ceiling. Writes saturate at \ensuremath{\sim}\qty{1.06}{\gibi\byte\per\second}
of SLC ingest even at QD=1. Mixing reads with writes is worse than either pure workload.

The read knee is at QD~\csvq{queue_depth_knee}{mode=read}{knee_qd} under an explicit
\qty{10}{\percent}-per-doubling rule. Beyond it, latency doubles with every doubling of QD for at most a
few percent more IOPS. $L/\text{QD}$ stays at 0.987--0.998. Little's Law holds by construction in a
closed loop, so this confirms that the harness keeps the queue full and the saturation is the device's.

Device state matters as much as configuration. A lean re-run on a freshly trimmed, idle drive found
the SLC cache much smaller than expected: a sequential fill hit the cliff after
\csvval[1]{sustained_summary}{cliff_gib}\,GiB instead of the \ensuremath{\sim}\qty{20}{\gibi\byte} we
had assumed, so the stop-at-cliff fill wrote only \csvval[1]{sustained_summary}{fill_gib}\,GiB. That
size is consistent with a dynamic cache that scales with the drive's free space, which we could not
control or measure because only the scratch partition was trimmed. We report it as the cache available
in this state, not as a fixed capacity. Past the cliff, sequential writes fall from
\csvval{sustained_summary}{burst_mibps} to \csvval{sustained_summary}{steady_mibps}\,MiB/s
($\ssdburstratio\times$), and 4\,KiB random writes run at about \num{15}k IOPS with
\qty{36}{\milli\second} per-second p99. A minute of idle restored at least \qty{1}{\gibi\byte} of cache
after this short fill, but nothing after the original run's \qty{60}{\gibi\byte} one. On the rested
drive, read tails widen with queue depth to p99.9/p50 $=\ssdtailratio{128}{p999}\times$ at QD=128.
Right after heavy writes they lost half their IOPS and reached $24\times$. Sequential reads of randomly
written data run $6$--$9\times$ slower whatever the queue depth. The write collapses seen inside the
original sweeps disappeared when the same points were re-measured after a rest. The NVMe temperature
peaked at \qty{61.9}{\celsius}, far below the drive's \qty{86.85}{\celsius} warning threshold, so we
saw no thermal effects.

The sharpest lessons are methodological. Without \code{O_DIRECT}, the same workload reported
page-cache fiction. The first dataset was taken on the HDD until the \code{rotational} flag gave it away.
A \enquote{sequential} read can be six to nine times slower depending only on how the data was written.
And on a QLC drive, \emph{what ran just before} changes write throughput by an order of magnitude and read
tails severalfold, and even a trimmed, idle drive offered an SLC cache far smaller than expected,
plausibly because its size depends on how full the drive is. A storage measurement can be trusted only if it bypasses the cache, verifies its
effective queue depth, controls the data's write history and the drive's recent workload, and confirms
which device it is talking to.
