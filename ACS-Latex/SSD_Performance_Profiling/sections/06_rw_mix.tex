\section{Read/Write Mix}\label{sec:rw-mix}
\rubric{Read/write and queue-depth analysis}{40}{Correct mix and queue-depth experiments, knee
identification and Little's Law reasoning (with \cref{sec:queue-depth}).}

\begin{hypothesis}
  At a fixed block size and queue depth, a mixed read/write workload should deliver \emph{fewer} total
  IOPS than either pure workload, not a weighted average: reads and writes contend for the controller and
  flash channels, and a read queued behind a slow program/erase inherits its latency. We expect pure
  writes to post the highest IOPS at QD=16 (write-buffer absorption) and the 50/50 mix to be the worst.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{knobs}
  Fixed       & \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} random; iodepth 16; one job; \code{O_DIRECT} \\
  Varied      & reads per 10 I/Os: 10 (100\% R), 7 (70/30), 5 (50/50), 0 (100\% W) \\
  Script      & \code{SSD_Performance_Profiling/scripts/run.sh rw_mix} \\
\end{knobs}

\subsection{Analysis}
The mix sweep confirms the hypothesis and then some. Pure writes are fastest
(\qty{185860}{} IOPS, \qty{85}{\micro\second} mean) because at QD=16 the controller coalesces them into
its SLC/DRAM buffer; pure reads reach \qty{104945}{} IOPS. But mixing is strictly worse than either pure
case: the 70/30 mix drops to \qty{85535}{} and the 50/50 mix to \qty{72119}{} IOPS---below even the
read-only number---because every write that lands mid-stream stalls the flash channel a read then queues
behind, and read-modify interference defeats the write buffer's coalescing. Latency tells the same story
from the tail: p99 climbs monotonically with the write fraction, from \qty{458}{\micro\second} (pure read)
through \qty{707}{} and \qty{995}{\micro\second} for the mixes, as background program/erase operations
inject outliers. The lesson is that a ``70\% read'' workload is not \num{0.7} of the read result plus
\num{0.3} of the write result; the interaction term is large and negative, which is exactly why the spec
requires measuring the mixes rather than interpolating them.
