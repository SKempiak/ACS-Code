\section{Intensity Sweep and the Throughput--Latency Knee}\label{sec:intensity}
\rubric{Throughput--latency knee}{35}{Clear knee identification, diminishing-returns discussion and a
Little's Law connection.}

\begin{hypothesis}
  A single core cannot saturate the DRAM controller because it can keep only a dozen or so cache-line
  requests in flight. Adding cores should raise delivered bandwidth until the dual-channel controller
  saturates---expected in the low tens of \unit{\gibi\byte\per\second}---after which more cores add
  almost nothing. By Little's Law, saturation needs tens of outstanding requests, more than one core
  provides.
\end{hypothesis}

\subsection{Little's Law check}
With offered concurrency rising, delivered line-throughput $\lambda$ and the service time $W$ of a line
bound the average number in flight, $L = \lambda W$ \cite{little1961proof}. Taking $W$ as the zero-queue
random DRAM latency (\qty{83.85}{\nano\second}, \cref{tab:zero-queue}) and $\lambda$ as the delivered
bandwidth divided by the \qty{64}{\byte} line size gives the memory-level parallelism the system must be
sustaining:

\subsection{Analysis}
Bandwidth climbs from \qty{14.4}{\gibi\byte\per\second} at one core to \qty{26.6}{\gibi\byte\per\second}
at four and then flattens, reaching \qty{30.2}{\gibi\byte\per\second} at ten---a textbook knee around
four to six cores. Beyond the knee each added core buys under \qty{1}{\gibi\byte\per\second}: the
dual-channel DDR4 controller, not the cores, is the bottleneck. The Little's Law column makes the cause
concrete: saturation requires \num{\sim}\num{42} concurrent line requests, far more than the \num{\sim}10
fill buffers of a single core, which is exactly why one core tops out at \qty{14}{\gibi\byte\per\second}
and multiple cores are needed to fill the pipe. Two honest caveats. First, the streaming kernel runs
under the prefetcher, so its \emph{effective} loaded latency is below the \qty{84}{\nano\second} random
figure; using the zero-queue value makes the $L$ column an upper-bound estimate of required concurrency,
not an exact occupancy. Second, because we measure aggregate throughput rather than an independent loaded
latency, the ``latency rises past the knee'' half of the tradeoff is inferred here; the SSD study
(Project~3) measures both axes directly and shows the knee quantitatively.
