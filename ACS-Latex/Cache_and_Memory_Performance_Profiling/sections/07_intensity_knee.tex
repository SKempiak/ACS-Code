\section{Intensity Sweep and the Throughput--Latency Knee}\label{sec:intensity}
\rubric{Throughput--latency knee}{35}{Clear knee identification, diminishing-returns discussion and a
Little's Law connection.}

% minipage: keep the hypothesis box in one piece (it must not split around Figure 4)
\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{hypothesis}
  A single core cannot saturate the DRAM controller because it can keep only about a dozen demand misses
  in flight (its L1 fill buffers). Adding load should raise delivered bandwidth until the memory
  controller saturates in the low tens of \unit{\gibi\byte\per\second} (assuming the usual two DDR4
  channels of this desktop platform); past that point extra load should
  buy almost no bandwidth but sharply raise the latency of every request (queueing). By Little's Law,
  saturation needs tens of outstanding lines---more than one core provides.
\end{hypothesis}
\end{minipage}

\begin{knobs}
  Generators  & \code{mem_loaded}: $T = 0 \ldots 9$ cores (all physical cores except CPU~2), each streaming its own \qty{128}{\mebi\byte} region with \qty{256}{\bit} loads; then $T=9$ throttled by an injection delay of 8--2048 empty iterations per line \\
  Probe       & random pointer chase over \qty{256}{\mebi\byte} on CPU~2, measured in the same window as the generators' throughput \\
  Known MLP   & $T$ cores each running $k$ independent random chases: $T=1$, $k = 1\ldots32$; $T = 2\ldots9$, $k=8$ \\
  Script      & \code{Cache_and_Memory_Performance_Profiling/scripts/run.sh intensity} \\
\end{knobs}

\paragraph{Method.} Each point reports \emph{both} axes from one measurement window: generator threads
publish progress counters, the main thread snapshots them, runs the latency probe for
\qty{200}{\milli\second}, and snapshots again. Throughput is all lines moved (generators plus probe) per
second; latency is the probe's time per dependent access---a demand read issued \emph{under} that load,
i.e.\ MLC's ``loaded latency''. $T=0$ is the unloaded latency of the same setup
($W_0 = \valqty{int_w0_ns}{\nano\second}$).

\paragraph{Knee criterion.} We define the knee as the first point, in order of \emph{offered} load
($T=0,1,\ldots,9$), at which delivered bandwidth reaches 95\% of the peak. Past it, extra load can buy at
most 5\% more bandwidth. The point of maximum throughput/latency (``power'') is a second criterion. As a
latency reference we also report where loaded latency first reaches $2W_0$, interpolated between the two
bracketing points; on this machine that happens only once bandwidth has already peaked, so it marks deep
saturation rather than the knee. All three come from the CSV (\code{derive.py}), not from the eye.

\subsection{Knee and diminishing returns}
The curve has the textbook shape (\cref{fig:intensity}, \cref{tab:littles-law}). The knee is at
$T=\val{int_sat_threads}$: bandwidth has climbed from the probe's own trickle to
\valqty{int_sat_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}, 95\% of the \valqty{int_peak_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} peak,
while latency has risen only from \valqty{int_w0_ns}{\nano\second} to \valqty{int_sat_ns}{\nano\second}
(\val{int_sat_ratio}$\times$). The throughput/latency maximum falls on the same point
($T=\val{int_power_threads}$, \valqty{int_power_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}, \valqty{int_power_ns}{\nano\second}),
so both criteria agree. Every core added after the knee buys almost no bandwidth: the peak is reached at
$T=\val{int_peak_threads}$, and $T=9$ still delivers \valqty{int_last_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}. Latency,
however, climbs steadily to \valqty{int_last_ns}{\nano\second}. It crosses $2W_0$ only at
\valqty{int_knee_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}, between $T=\val{int_knee_prevthreads}$ and
$T=\val{int_knee_threads}$, i.e.\ at the bandwidth peak itself. (In the injection-delay sweep at $T=9$ the
crossing is at \valqty{int_kneed_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}.) The memory-controller counters confirm the
throughput axis: at $T=4$ and $T=9$ measured DRAM traffic is \val{int_t4_imc_ratio}$\times$ and
\val{int_t9_imc_ratio}$\times$ the delivered bandwidth. Beyond the knee the memory controller, not the
cores, is the bottleneck: requests no longer finish faster, they queue.
\paragraph{Theoretical DRAM peak.} \code{dmidecode} (\code{ACS-Code/env/dmidecode_memory.txt}) shows
two \qty{32}{\gibi\byte} DDR4-3200 DIMMs, one in each of the two channels, running at
\qty{2933}{MT/s}. The theoretical peak is therefore
$2~\text{channels}\times\qty{8}{\byte}\times\qty{2933}{MT/s}=\qty{46.9}{\giga\byte\per\second}
=\qty{43.7}{\gibi\byte\per\second}$. The measured peak of \valqty{int_peak_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}
is \qty{73}{\percent} of it, and the knee at \valqty{int_sat_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} is
\qty{70}{\percent}. Reaching roughly 70--75\% of the theoretical figure is the usual practical limit for
DDR4 reads, because of refresh, bank conflicts and read/write bus turnarounds.

\paragraph{Comparison with Intel MLC.} MLC's \texttt{-{}-loaded\_latency} sweep (nine load cores, injection
delay from 20\,000 down to 0 cycles, latency thread on CPU~2) is plotted in \cref{fig:intensity}. It
starts lower, at \valqty{mlc_ll_w0_ns}{\nano\second}, for the access-pattern reasons discussed in
\cref{sec:zero-queue}. By the same 95\% rule, MLC's knee is at \valqty{mlc_sat_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} and
\valqty{mlc_sat_ns}{\nano\second} (peak \valqty{mlc_ll_peak_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}, injection delay
\val{mlc_sat_delay}), against our \valqty{int_sat_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}. MLC's own latency doubles at
\valqty{mlc_knee_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}. At full load it
reports \valqty{mlc_ll_d0_ns}{\nano\second}. The two instruments agree on the ceiling (within 2\%) and on the
shape: latency stays near its idle value until the controller approaches
\qtyrange{30}{32}{\gibi\byte\per\second} and then rises about threefold with almost no further bandwidth.

\subsection{Little's Law with measured latency}
For every load level we compute the mean number of lines in flight, $L = \lambda W$
\cite{little1961proof}, with $\lambda$ the delivered line rate and $W$ the \emph{measured} loaded latency
of that same window. At the knee ($T=\val{int_sat_threads}$), $L=\valnum{int_sat_L}$ lines. For comparison, $L_0 = \lambda W_0$ uses the unloaded latency, the usual shortcut
when loaded latency is not measured. $L_0$ rises to \valnum{int_L0max} lines at saturation and then stays flat: that is the
concurrency the memory system can actually \emph{serve} at about \qty{30}{\gibi\byte\per\second}. $L$, in
contrast, keeps growing to \valnum{int_Lmax} lines at $T=9$; the difference $L - L_0$ is requests waiting in
queues, which is exactly why latency rises without any bandwidth gain.

\paragraph{What the known-concurrency sweep shows.} Computing $L=\lambda W$ with $W$ taken as each
chain's own step time gives $L/N$ between \val{mlp_LN_min} and \val{mlp_LN_max}. That is \emph{not} a
validation of Little's Law: with that $W$, $\lambda W = N$ holds by construction, so it only confirms the
progress accounting. The informative quantity is
$L_{\text{mem}} = \lambda W_{\text{mem}}$, where $W_{\text{mem}}$ is the latency of a memory request measured
at the same time by the probe (\valqty{mlp_t1_wmem}{\nano\second} on average with one generator core). One
core reaches its best random-read bandwidth, \valqty{mlp_t1_peak_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}, at
$k=\val{mlp_t1_peak_k}$, and its $L_{\text{mem}}$ never exceeds \valnum{mlp_t1_Lmem_max} lines
(\cref{fig:mlp}). Chains beyond that do not reach memory any sooner: they wait inside the core, so the
per-chain step time grows instead (to \valqty{mlp_t1_last_ns}{\nano\second} at $k=\val{mlp_t1_last_k}$).
About nine outstanding demand misses per core is consistent with the 12 L1D fill buffers of this
Skylake-family core. One point, $k=12$, is an outlier (slower than both $k=10$ and $k=16$, with the same memory latency); we
did not resolve this generator artifact and use the envelope. With $k=8$ per core, adding
cores raises bandwidth to \valqty{mlp_peak_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} at $T=\val{mlp_peak_T}$
($L_{\text{mem}}=\valnum{mlp_peak_Lmem}$, $W_{\text{mem}}=\valqty{mlp_peak_wmem}{\nano\second}$). Random demand
reads thus reach only about 60\% of the streaming peak: without prefetching, nine cores of this kind keep
about seven lines each in flight and the memory system queues long before it is fully used.

\paragraph{Reconciling one core's streaming concurrency with the fill buffers.} The streaming sweep gives
one core $L=\valnum{int_t1_L}$ lines in flight at \valqty{int_t1_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}
(\cref{tab:littles-law}), about three times the \valnum{mlp_t1_Lmem_max} demand misses one core sustains.
There is no contradiction: in a sequential stream most lines are brought in by the L2 hardware prefetcher,
whose requests are tracked in the L2's own request queue rather than in the L1 fill buffers, so a streaming
core keeps more lines in flight than a core issuing random demand misses. Quoting the fill-buffer count
as the per-core limit is correct only for demand misses; the two measurements quantify both limits. The \valnum{int_L0max} lines the
controller serves at saturation are about two streaming cores' worth of concurrency (with queueing, four
cores are needed to reach 95\% of peak), but six random-access cores' worth.
