\section{Read/Write Mix}\label{sec:rw-mix}

\begin{hypothesis}
  Pure reads should reach the highest \emph{demand} bandwidth. Every written line first needs a
  read-for-ownership (RFO) and later a write-back, so a written line costs about two lines of DRAM traffic
  and adds read/write bus turnarounds. Demand bandwidth should therefore fall as the write fraction grows,
  while estimated DRAM traffic stays closer to constant; and loaded latency should be highest for the
  write-heavy mixes, whose write-backs compete with the probe's reads.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{knobs}
  Fixed       & \qty{128}{\mebi\byte} per generator core; sequential; ordinary (write-allocate) full-line stores; \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} pages \\
  Varied      & reads per 10 \emph{lines}: 10 (100/0), 7 (70/30), 5 (50/50), 0 (0/100); $T = 1$ and $9$ generator cores \\
  Latency     & concurrent random pointer-chase probe on CPU~2 (\code{mem_loaded}); $T=0$ gives the unloaded value \\
  Script      & \code{Cache_and_Memory_Performance_Profiling/scripts/run.sh rw_mix} \\
\end{knobs}

\paragraph{Mix definition.} Of every 10 consecutive \qty{64}{\byte} lines, the first $R$ are read and
the remaining $10-R$ are fully overwritten. Mixing at \emph{line} granularity makes the ratio hold for the
traffic the memory system sees. Mixing individual \qty{8}{\byte} elements instead is a
methodological trap: with 7 reads and 3 writes per 10 elements, every line of the source is read
\emph{and} every line of the destination written, so a nominal ``70/30'' moves about three lines per
useful line and measures the access pattern, not the mix (\cref{sec:ai-use}). ``Demand'' bandwidth counts the bytes the program reads or writes; ``est.\ traffic''
adds one RFO line per written line (written lines count twice); ``DRAM (IMC)'' is the read plus write
traffic measured by the memory-controller counters over the same window.

\subsection{Analysis}
With nine generator cores, demand bandwidth is \valqty{rw_10_t9_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} for 100\%
reads, \valqty{rw_7_t9_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} at 70/30, \valqty{rw_5_t9_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}
at 50/50 and \valqty{rw_0_t9_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} for 100\% writes, falling with the write fraction as
predicted. Counting one RFO per written line, the estimated DRAM traffic is
\valqty{rw_10_t9_traffic}{\gibi\byte\per\second}, \valqty{rw_7_t9_traffic}{\gibi\byte\per\second},
\valqty{rw_5_t9_traffic}{\gibi\byte\per\second} and \valqty{rw_0_t9_traffic}{\gibi\byte\per\second}. The
memory-controller counters measure \valqty{rw_10_t9_imc}{\gibi\byte\per\second},
\valqty{rw_7_t9_imc}{\gibi\byte\per\second}, \valqty{rw_5_t9_imc}{\gibi\byte\per\second} and
\valqty{rw_0_t9_imc}{\gibi\byte\per\second} (within about 1\% of the estimate at every mix), against an idle background of \valqty{cc_imc_rd_idle}{\gibi\byte\per\second}. The RFO model is
therefore exact: for pure writes the controller reads \valqty{rw_0_t9_imcrd}{\gibi\byte\per\second} (the RFOs)
and writes \valqty{rw_0_t9_imcwr}{\gibi\byte\per\second}. Total DRAM traffic stays at the controller's ceiling
of about \qty{30}{\gibi\byte\per\second} whatever the mix; MLC's peak-injection bandwidth agrees
(\valqty{mlc_pk_allreads_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} all reads, \valqty{mlc_pk_r31_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}
at 3:1, \valqty{mlc_pk_r11_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} at 1:1; MLC reports
\valnum{mlc_pk_allreads_mbs}~MB/s for all reads, $1~\text{MB} = 10^6$\,B). The cost of writes is
therefore the RFO, not a loss of controller efficiency.

The concurrently measured probe latency is \valqty{rw_10_t9_ns}{\nano\second},
\valqty{rw_7_t9_ns}{\nano\second}, \valqty{rw_5_t9_ns}{\nano\second} and \valqty{rw_0_t9_ns}{\nano\second}
for the four mixes (unloaded: \valqty{rw_unloaded_ns}{\nano\second}): at the same total traffic, write-heavy
mixes double read latency. Write-backs and RFOs occupy the same channels and queues as the probe's reads,
and read/write bus turnarounds lengthen every read's wait.

A single generator core ($T=1$) is not controller-bound: demand bandwidth falls only slightly from
\valqty{rw_10_t1_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} (reads) to \valqty{rw_0_t1_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} (writes),
while measured DRAM traffic grows from \valqty{rw_10_t1_imc}{\gibi\byte\per\second} to
\valqty{rw_0_t1_imc}{\gibi\byte\per\second}. A core can keep more lines moving when part of its stream is
stores, which retire without waiting for their RFO. The probe barely notices one core:
\val{rw_t1_ns_min}--\valqty{rw_t1_ns_max}{\nano\second} across the four mixes. As named-kernel references on all
ten cores, \code{copy} delivers \valqty{rw_ref_copy}{\gibi\byte\per\second} and \code{triad}
\valqty{rw_ref_triad}{\gibi\byte\per\second} of demand bandwidth. The counters show their RFOs too: copy reads
\valqty{rw_ref_copy_imcrd}{\gibi\byte\per\second} and writes \valqty{rw_ref_copy_imcwr}{\gibi\byte\per\second}
(2:1, source plus RFO), and triad reads \valqty{rw_ref_triad_imcrd}{\gibi\byte\per\second} and writes
\valqty{rw_ref_triad_imcwr}{\gibi\byte\per\second} (3:1). Non-temporal stores, which skip the RFO, were out of
scope for the required mixes.
