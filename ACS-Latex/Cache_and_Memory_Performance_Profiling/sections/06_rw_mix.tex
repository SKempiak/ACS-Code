\section{Read/Write Mix}\label{sec:rw-mix}

\begin{hypothesis}
  Pure reads should reach the highest bandwidth. Any write fraction should cost extra, because an
  ordinary store first issues a read-for-ownership (RFO) of the target line, so writes move roughly twice
  the DRAM traffic per byte stored and add read/write turnaround on the bus. We therefore expect mixed
  streams to fall \emph{below} both pure read and pure write.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{knobs}
  Fixed       & 10 threads (one per physical core); random-free unit stride; \qty{1.25}{\gibi\byte} total \\
  Varied      & reads per 10 accesses: 10 (100/0), 7 (70/30), 5 (50/50), 0 (0/100) \\
  Script      & \code{Cache_and_Memory_Performance_Profiling/scripts/run.sh rwmix} \\
\end{knobs}

\subsection{Analysis}
The 10-thread aggregate is \qty{27.2}{\gibi\byte\per\second} for 100\% reads but only
\qty{8.1}{\gibi\byte\per\second} at 70/30 and \qty{7.6}{\gibi\byte\per\second} at 50/50, recovering to
\qty{9.7}{\gibi\byte\per\second} at 100\% writes. The dip at 50/50 is the predicted effect: a mixed
stream pays both for RFO traffic on every store and for frequent read$\leftrightarrow$write turnaround on
the DDR bus, so it underperforms either pure direction. Pure writes beat the mixes because, with no
interleaved reads, the controller can batch same-direction transactions. As same-kernel references, the
\code{copy} (\qty{16.3}{\gibi\byte\per\second}) and \code{triad} (\qty{19.8}{\gibi\byte\per\second})
streaming kernels bracket these mixes and show the effect of counting RFO traffic. One honest caveat:
our \code{rwmix} kernel reads into a single accumulator and stores it, so the absolute mix numbers carry
a read$\to$write dependency and are best read as a \emph{relative} comparison across mixes rather than as
peak bandwidth (\cref{sec:anomalies}).
