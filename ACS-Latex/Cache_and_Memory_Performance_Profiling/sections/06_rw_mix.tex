\section{Read/Write Mix}\label{sec:rw-mix}

\begin{hypothesis}
  Pure reads should reach the highest \emph{demand} bandwidth. Every written line first needs a
  read-for-ownership (RFO) and later a write-back, so a written line costs about two lines of DRAM traffic
  and adds read/write bus turnarounds. Demand bandwidth should therefore fall as the write fraction grows,
  while estimated DRAM traffic stays closer to constant; and loaded latency should be highest for the
  write-heavy mixes, whose write-backs compete with the probe's reads.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{knobs}
  Fixed       & \qty{128}{\mebi\byte} per generator core; sequential; ordinary (write-allocate) full-line stores; \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} pages \\
  Varied      & reads per 10 \emph{lines}: 10 (100/0), 7 (70/30), 5 (50/50), 0 (0/100); $T = 1$ and $9$ generator cores \\
  Latency     & concurrent random pointer-chase probe on CPU~2 (\code{mem_loaded}); $T=0$ gives the unloaded value \\
  Script      & \code{Cache_and_Memory_Performance_Profiling/scripts/run.sh rw_mix} \\
\end{knobs}

\paragraph{Mix definition.} Of every 10 consecutive \qty{64}{\byte} lines, the first $R$ are read and
the remaining $10-R$ are fully overwritten. Mixing at \emph{line} granularity makes the ratio hold for the
traffic the memory system sees. Mixing individual \qty{8}{\byte} elements instead is a
methodological trap: with 7 reads and 3 writes per 10 elements, every line of the source is read
\emph{and} every line of the destination written, so a nominal ``70/30'' moves about three lines per
useful line and measures the access pattern, not the mix (\cref{sec:ai-use}). ``Demand'' bandwidth counts the bytes the program reads or writes; ``est.\ traffic''
adds one RFO line per written line (written lines count twice).

\subsection{Analysis}
With nine generator cores, demand bandwidth is \valqty{rw_10_t9_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} for 100\%
reads, \valqty{rw_7_t9_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} at 70/30, \valqty{rw_5_t9_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}
at 50/50 and \valqty{rw_0_t9_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} for 100\% writes. Counting the RFO, estimated
DRAM traffic is \valqty{rw_10_t9_traffic}{\gibi\byte\per\second}, \valqty{rw_7_t9_traffic}{\gibi\byte\per\second},
\valqty{rw_5_t9_traffic}{\gibi\byte\per\second} and \valqty{rw_0_t9_traffic}{\gibi\byte\per\second}
respectively. Traffic is therefore roughly constant at the controller's ceiling for pure reads, 70/30
and pure writes---each written line costs its demand line plus one RFO, and the controller delivers about
the same number of lines either way---but drops by about a fifth at 50/50, where the bus has to turn
around between reads and writes most often. Demand bandwidth falls monotonically with the write fraction,
as predicted. The concurrently measured probe latency is \valqty{rw_10_t9_ns}{\nano\second},
\valqty{rw_7_t9_ns}{\nano\second}, \valqty{rw_5_t9_ns}{\nano\second} and \valqty{rw_0_t9_ns}{\nano\second}
for the same four mixes (unloaded: \valqty{rw_unloaded_ns}{\nano\second}); write-backs occupy the same
channels and queues as the probe's reads, so a write-heavy stream raises read latency even at lower
demand bandwidth. A single generator core ($T=1$) is not controller-bound and behaves differently: its
demand bandwidth is \valqty{rw_10_t1_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} for reads, \emph{highest} at 70/30
(\valqty{rw_7_t1_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}) and \valqty{rw_0_t1_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} for writes, while
its estimated traffic grows from \valqty{rw_10_t1_traffic}{\gibi\byte\per\second} to
\valqty{rw_0_t1_traffic}{\gibi\byte\per\second}. A plausible explanation, not tested here, is that stores retire into the store
buffer without waiting for their RFO, so a core can keep more lines moving when part of its stream is writes than when every line is a
load it must wait for. The probe barely notices one core: \val{rw_t1_ns_min}--\valqty{rw_t1_ns_max}{\nano\second} across the four mixes. As named-kernel references on all
ten cores, \code{copy} delivers \valqty{rw_ref_copy}{\gibi\byte\per\second} and \code{triad}
\valqty{rw_ref_triad}{\gibi\byte\per\second} (demand bytes; 1:1 and 2:1 read:write). Non-temporal
stores, which skip the RFO, were out of scope for the required mixes.
