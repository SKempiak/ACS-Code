\section{Zero-Queue Baselines}\label{sec:zero-queue}
\rubric{Zero-queue baselines}{25}{Methodologically sound per-level latency measurements with clear units and caveats.}

\begin{hypothesis}
  With a single dependent access in flight, latency should rise in steps at the L1 (\qty{32}{\kibi\byte}),
  L2 (\qty{256}{\kibi\byte}) and L3 (\qty{20}{\mebi\byte}) capacities. We expect roughly 4--5 cycles for
  L1, \ensuremath{\sim}\num{12}~cycles for L2, a few tens of cycles for L3, and \ensuremath{\sim}\qty{80}{\nano\second}
  (hundreds of cycles) for DRAM, matching Skylake-family numbers.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{knobs}
  Fixed       & one thread pinned to CPU~2; random (Sattolo) pointer chase; one node per \qty{64}{\byte} line \\
  Varied      & working-set size inside each level's plateau window; page size (DRAM only); method (primary vs.\ cross-check) \\
  Script      & \code{Cache_and_Memory_Performance_Profiling/scripts/run.sh zero_queue} \\
\end{knobs}

The ring is a single random cycle over one node per cache line, so each load depends on the previous one
and exactly one request is outstanding---zero-queue latency. Cycles are converted with the
fixed \qty{2.8}{\giga\hertz} core clock, which the cycle counter confirms (\cref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Plateau rule.} A level's latency is \emph{not} read at its capacity edge, where the set is
partly spilling (e.g.\ \qty{256}{\kibi\byte} is a transition point, not an L2 point). Instead,
\code{derive.py} takes the median, over all measured sizes $s$ with
$2\,C_{\text{prev}} \le s \le 0.75\,C_{\text{level}}$, of the per-size medians ($C_{\text{prev}} =
\qty{4}{\kibi\byte}$ for L1); DRAM uses every size $\ge 4\times$ the L3. The same values feed the
abstract, introduction and conclusion through generated macros, so they cannot disagree.

\subsection{Analysis}
Latency is flat at \valqty{zq_L1_ns}{\nano\second} (\valnum{zq_L1_cyc}~cycles) while the set fits in L1.
The L2 plateau with \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} pages is \valqty{zq_L2_ns}{\nano\second} (\valnum{zq_L2_cyc}~cycles),
the median of sizes that measure \val{zq_L2_min}--\valqty{zq_L2_max}{\nano\second}. This headline value is
\emph{not} the L2 hit latency: the high end comes from \qty{128}{\kibi\byte} and \qty{192}{\kibi\byte}, where
\qty{4}{\kibi\byte} pages already cause L2 conflict misses (\cref{sec:working-set}). With \qty{2}{\mebi\byte}
pages the same plateau sizes give \valqty{zq_L2_hugens}{\nano\second} (about 12 cycles), as does the low end
of the \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} range; that is the L2 hit latency. Latency spans
\val{zq_L3_min}--\valqty{zq_L3_max}{\nano\second} across the L3 (median \valqty{zq_L3_ns}{\nano\second});
L3 latency is not a single number, because larger sets also miss the second-level TLB. It reaches
\valqty{zq_DRAM_ns}{\nano\second} (\valnum{zq_DRAM_cyc}~cycles) in DRAM. With \qty{2}{\mebi\byte} pages the
DRAM figure drops to \valqty{zq_DRAM_hugens}{\nano\second}; the \valqty{zq_DRAM_walk_ns}{\nano\second}
difference is the page walk that every random \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} access beyond the TLB reach pays
(\cref{sec:tlb}). Our second method agrees within \val{zq_xdiff_absmax}\% at every level: the largest
difference is on the L2 plateau, where it reads \val{zq_L2_xdiff_abs}\% lower, and on DRAM it differs by
\val{zq_DRAM_xdiff_abs}\%. Against the hypothesis, L1 (5 cycles), L2 (12 cycles without conflicts) and L3
match. DRAM with \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} pages is slower than the \ensuremath{\sim}\qty{80}{\nano\second} we
expected, and the \qty{2}{\mebi\byte}-page value shows that the excess is mostly the page walk.

\paragraph{Comparison with Intel MLC.} MLC agrees with our \qty{4}{\kibi\byte}-page L2 plateau
(\valqty{zq_L2_mlcns}{\nano\second}, \valnum{zq_L2_mlcdiff}\% relative to ours) but is lower at the other
levels: L1 \valqty{zq_L1_mlcns}{\nano\second} (\valnum{zq_L1_mlcdiff}\%), L3
\valqty{zq_L3_mlcns}{\nano\second} (\valnum{zq_L3_mlcdiff}\%), and DRAM \valqty{zq_DRAM_mlcns}{\nano\second}
(\valnum{zq_DRAM_mlcdiff}\%). The differences follow from how the tools walk
memory, not from noise:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{L1.} MLC's 4 clocks versus our 5 cycles fit the known 4-cycle load-to-use latency for a plain
        pointer dereference. Our index ring forms \code{ring[p]} with a scaled-index address, which costs
        one extra cycle.
  \item \textbf{DRAM.} MLC returns the same \valqty{mlc_DRAM_huge_ns}{\nano\second} with and without
        \code{-L} (\qty{2}{\mebi\byte} pages). So its random walk takes no page walks, consistent with its
        \code{-D} random-access range of \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} (the default): consecutive accesses stay
        inside one page. Our ring is random over the whole buffer, so even with \qty{2}{\mebi\byte} pages MLC's
        figure is \val{mlc_DRAM_huge_below}\% below ours (\valqty{zq_DRAM_hugens}{\nano\second}), and with
        \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} pages our ring also pays the walk. Accesses that stay close together should also hit open DRAM rows more often.
\end{itemize}
MLC therefore measures a best-case local latency; our numbers are the cost of truly random access. Both
are valid, and we keep ours as the zero-queue baseline because the rest of the report (AMAT, TLB) needs
fully random access.

Caveats: the L3 and DRAM figures are a single-core view of shared resources on an otherwise idle
system. Sequential ``latency'' (\cref{sec:pattern}) is prefetcher throughput, which is why the chase is
randomized.
