\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The hierarchy behaves as its capacities predict, and the counters show \emph{why} each step occurs.
Zero-queue latency is \valqty{zq_L1_ns}{\nano\second} (\valnum{zq_L1_cyc}~cycles) in L1,
\valqty{zq_L2_ns}{\nano\second} in L2, \valqty{zq_L3_ns}{\nano\second} in L3 and
\valqty{zq_DRAM_ns}{\nano\second} in DRAM, of which \valqty{zq_DRAM_walk_ns}{\nano\second} is page walking
that \qty{2}{\mebi\byte} pages remove. Concurrency is the other half of the story. Measured under load,
latency stays near its unloaded value until \valqty{int_knee_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} and then climbs
steeply towards the \valqty{int_peak_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} ceiling: past the knee, more cores buy
queueing, not bandwidth. With measured latency, Little's Law separates the \valnum{int_L0max} lines the memory
system serves at saturation from up to \valnum{int_Lmax} lines that merely queue; one core sustains at most
\valnum{mlp_t1_Lmem_max} random demand misses, but far more lines when the prefetcher streams; and a
counter-fitted AMAT model shows random SAXPY paying \valqty{amat_rand_aD}{\nano\second} per DRAM miss---the
DRAM latency divided by \valnum{amat_rand_mlp}-way overlap. The lessons are about measurement discipline as
much as hardware: a bandwidth benchmark must out-size the LLC or it measures cache reuse
(\cref{sec:pitfall}); a latency benchmark must randomize or it measures the prefetcher; a knee must come from
measured latency, not throughput alone; a read/write mix must be defined at line granularity; and a
huge-page study must verify its pages---in both directions (\cref{sec:tlb}).
