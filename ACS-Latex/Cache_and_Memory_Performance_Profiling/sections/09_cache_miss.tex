\section{Cache-Miss Impact}\label{sec:cache-miss}

\begin{hypothesis}
  As the SAXPY footprint grows past each cache, time per element should rise in proportion to the misses
  per element at each level. Sequential traversal should rise gently---the prefetcher turns misses into
  hits---while a random permutation should rise sharply once the set leaves cache. Because independent
  iterations overlap their misses, the effective cost of a DRAM miss should be well below the zero-queue
  DRAM latency.
\end{hypothesis}

  Varied      & $N = 2^{8} \ldots 2^{24}$ (footprint $16N$ bytes); order (sequential, random permutation) \\

\subsection{Analysis}
Sequential SAXPY rises from \valqty{cm_seq_min_ns}{\nano\second} per element in L1 to
\valqty{cm_seq_max_ns}{\nano\second} in DRAM, a \val{cm_seq_span}$\times$ span, with only
\val{cm_seq_max_llc_miss} demand LLC misses per element at the largest size: the prefetcher has already
brought most lines in. Random traversal rises from \valqty{cm_rand_min_ns}{\nano\second} to
\valqty{cm_rand_max_ns}{\nano\second}, a \val{cm_rand_span}$\times$ span, with \val{cm_rand_max_l1d_miss}
L1D misses, \val{cm_rand_max_llc_miss} LLC misses and \val{cm_rand_max_dtlb_miss} page walks per element at
$N=2^{24}$ (each element touches a line of $x$, a line of $y$, plus page-walk reads). At DRAM sizes the
random curve is \val{cm_dram_gap}$\times$ the sequential one: that gap \emph{is} the miss cost the
prefetcher hides for regular code.

\subsection{AMAT fitted to measured miss counts}
Classic AMAT, $t_{L1} + m_{L1}\bigl(t_{L2} + m_{L2}(t_{L3} + m_{L3}\,t_{\text{DRAM}})\bigr)$, assumes
misses are serviced one at a time. We instead fit its per-element form to the counters, separately for each
order:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amat-fit}
  t_{\text{elem}} = t_0 + a_2\,h_{L2} + a_3\,h_{L3} + a_D\,m_D ,
\end{equation}
where $h_{L2}$ = L1D misses $-$ LLC references (served by L2), $h_{L3}$ = LLC references $-$ LLC misses
(served by L3) and $m_D$ = LLC misses (served by DRAM), all per element; $t_0$ absorbs the L1-hit work.
Ordinary least squares over the 17 footprints gives, for random order,
$t_0=\valqty{amat_rand_t0}{\nano\second}$, $a_2=\valqty{amat_rand_a2}{\nano\second}$,
$a_3=\valqty{amat_rand_a3}{\nano\second}$, $a_D=\valqty{amat_rand_aD}{\nano\second}$ ($R^2=\val{amat_rand_r2}$);
for sequential order $a_D=\valqty{amat_seq_aD}{\nano\second}$ ($R^2=\val{amat_seq_r2}$). The fitted $a_D$ is
the \emph{exposed} cost of one DRAM miss. Dividing the zero-queue DRAM latency by it gives the average
number of DRAM misses overlapped: \val{amat_rand_mlp} for random and \val{amat_seq_mlp} for sequential
SAXPY. For random order the same reading holds at every level: the zero-queue L2 and L3 latencies are
\val{amat_rand_ovlL2}$\times$ and \val{amat_rand_ovlL3}$\times$ the fitted $a_2$ and $a_3$. All three overlap
factors (7.5--9) match the \valnum{mlp_t1_Lmem_max} demand misses one core keeps in flight in
\cref{sec:intensity}: independent SAXPY iterations fill the same fill buffers that the chase experiment
measured, and AMAT with serialised misses would overpredict the DRAM-resident runtime about
\val{amat_rand_mlp}-fold. Sequential order overlaps more (\val{amat_seq_mlp} for DRAM) because the misses
that remain after prefetching arrive in long, predictable runs. Caveats: all coefficients came out positive,
but the fit is a four-parameter regression on 17 footprints, so the individual L2/L3 terms are only roughly
determined; the random-order fit has its largest relative error, \val{amat_rand_maxrelerr}\%, at the smallest
and mid-size footprints, where one shared $t_0$ cannot describe the index-load and loop overhead (sequential:
\val{amat_seq_maxrelerr}\%). The page-walk cost is folded into $a_D$ because walks and DRAM misses grow
together in this sweep (\cref{sec:tlb} separates them).
