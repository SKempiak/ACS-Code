\begin{abstract}
  We profile the cache and memory hierarchy of an Intel i9-10900F with four purpose-built
  microbenchmarks and user-space hardware counters. Zero-queue pointer-chase latency, taken on each
  level's plateau by a stated rule, is \valqty{zq_L1_ns}{\nano\second} in L1 (\valnum{zq_L1_cyc}~cycles),
  \valqty{zq_L2_ns}{\nano\second} in L2 (\valnum{zq_L2_cyc}~cycles), \valqty{zq_L3_ns}{\nano\second} in
  the \qty{20}{\mebi\byte} L3 and \valqty{zq_DRAM_ns}{\nano\second} in DRAM with \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} pages
  (\valqty{zq_DRAM_hugens}{\nano\second} with \qty{2}{\mebi\byte} pages). A loaded-latency probe running
  concurrently with up to nine streaming cores traces the throughput--latency curve directly: latency
  doubles at \valqty{int_knee_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} (\valnum{int_knee_frac}\% of the
  \valqty{int_peak_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} peak), and Little's Law with the \emph{measured} loaded
  latency puts \valnum{int_knee_lines} lines in flight at the knee, against \valnum{int_L0max} that the
  memory system actually serves---the rest is queueing. A known-concurrency chase shows one core keeps at
  most \valnum{mlp_t1_Lmem_max} random demand misses in flight, however many independent chains it runs. Counter-based AMAT fits explain the
  \valnum{cm_rand_span}$\times$ random-SAXPY slowdown, and \qty{2}{\mebi\byte} pages---verified as
  granted---remove the page-walk cost past the \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} TLB reach. We close with the
  cache-reuse bandwidth pitfall, where a \valqty{pit_small_kib}{\kibi\byte} buffer reports
  \valqty{pit_small_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} of nonexistent ``DRAM'' bandwidth.
\end{abstract}
