\begin{abstract}
  We profile the cache and memory hierarchy of an Intel i9-10900F with four purpose-built
  microbenchmarks, user-space core counters, system-wide memory-controller counters and Intel MLC.
  Zero-queue pointer-chase latency, taken on each level's plateau by a stated rule, is
  \valqty{zq_L1_ns}{\nano\second} in L1 (\valnum{zq_L1_cyc}~cycles), \valqty{zq_L2_ns}{\nano\second} in L2,
  \valqty{zq_L3_ns}{\nano\second} in the \qty{20}{\mebi\byte} L3 and \valqty{zq_DRAM_ns}{\nano\second} in DRAM
  with \qty{4}{\kibi\byte} pages (\valqty{zq_DRAM_hugens}{\nano\second} with \qty{2}{\mebi\byte} pages). MLC
  reports a lower \valqty{zq_DRAM_mlcns}{\nano\second}, because its random walk stays within a page. A
  loaded-latency probe running concurrently with up to nine streaming cores traces the throughput--latency
  curve directly. The knee is at four cores: \valqty{int_sat_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}, 95\% of peak, at
  \val{int_sat_ratio}$\times$ the unloaded latency (MLC's own sweep: \valqty{mlc_sat_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second}).
  More cores add queueing but no bandwidth, and latency more than doubles. With measured
  latency, Little's Law separates the \valnum{int_L0max} lines the controller serves from up to
  \valnum{int_Lmax} that queue, and one core sustains at most \valnum{mlp_t1_Lmem_max} random demand misses.
  Counter-based AMAT fits explain the \valnum{cm_rand_span}$\times$ random-SAXPY slowdown; verified
  \qty{2}{\mebi\byte} pages remove a page-walk cost of up to \valqty{tlb_gap_65536_ns}{\nano\second} per access;
  and the memory-controller counters match the read-for-ownership traffic model within 1\% at full load
  (4--5.5\% below it with one core).
  We close with the cache-reuse bandwidth pitfall, where a \valqty{pit_small_kib}{\kibi\byte} buffer reports
  \valqty{pit_small_gib}{\gibi\byte\per\second} of nonexistent ``DRAM'' bandwidth.
\end{abstract}
