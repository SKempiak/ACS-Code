\section{Scalar vs.\ SIMD Baseline}\label{sec:baseline}
\rubric{Baseline, correctness, reproducibility}{15}{Speedup and achieved throughput for every kernel
across working-set sizes.}

\begin{hypothesis}
  For compute-friendly, unit-stride kernels (SAXPY, elementwise multiply, stencil) we expect the
  \code{native} speedup to approach the float32 lane count ($8\times$) while data is L1/L2-resident,
  and to fall toward $1\times$ once the working set is DRAM-resident and all variants share the same
  bandwidth. \code{autovec} (\num{4} lanes) should track at roughly half the \code{native} speedup.
  The dot product should barely speed up at any size because its reduction does not reassociate
  (\cref{sec:vectorization}).
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{knobs}
  Fixed       & float32; unit stride; aligned arrays; one thread pinned to CPU~2 \\
  Varied      & kernel; variant (scalar, autovec, native); $N = 2^{8} \ldots 2^{25}$ \\
  Repetitions & 12 per point; median, IQR error bars \\
  Script      & \code{SIMD_Advantage_Profiling/scripts/run.sh baseline} \\
\end{knobs}

\subsection{Analysis}
At $N=\num{4096}$ (L1-resident) the \code{native} speedups are SAXPY $5.8\times$ (\qty{35}{\giga\flop\per\second}),
stencil $3.5\times$ (\qty{30}{\giga\flop\per\second}), elementwise multiply $3.3\times$, and dot
$1.04\times$; \code{autovec} is close to half the \code{native} figure. None reaches the full
$8\times$ lane count even in cache because each vector FMA still needs one or two \qty{256}{bit}
loads and (for SAXPY/mul/stencil) a store, so the loop is load/store-port-bound well before it is
FLOP-bound.

The three streaming kernels behave as predicted and the two outliers are informative.
\emph{Elementwise multiply} does only one FLOP per element over the same three memory references as
SAXPY, so it has the lowest arithmetic intensity and the smallest cache-resident speedup of the
streaming kernels. The \emph{stencil} has the highest FLOP count (5) and reuses $x_{i-1},x_i,x_{i+1}$
across iterations, so it stays the most compute-bound and keeps a $2\times$ advantage even in DRAM
(\cref{fig:baseline-speedup}). The \emph{dot product} is the reduction case from
\cref{sec:vectorization}: packed multiplies, serial sum, $\approx1\times$ throughout.

By $N=\num{16777216}$ (\qty{128}{\mebi\byte}, far past L3) every speedup has collapsed---SAXPY
$1.37\times$, multiply $1.24\times$, dot $1.02\times$---and \cref{fig:baseline-throughput} shows why:
scalar and native SAXPY converge to the same $\approx\qty{5}{\giga\flop\per\second}$ plateau. The
curves bend downward exactly at the L1 ($N\approx\num{4096}$) and L2 ($N\approx\num{32768}$)
boundaries; \cref{sec:locality} resolves those transitions in detail and \cref{sec:roofline} ties the
DRAM plateau to measured bandwidth.
