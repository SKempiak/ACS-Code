\section{Negative Example}\label{sec:negative}
\rubric{AI-use transparency and failure case}{10}{A meaningful negative or misleading example, verified
and explained (with \cref{sec:ai-use}).}

\begin{hypothesis}
  A naive benchmark---or a quickly generated one that only ever tests a small array---would conclude
  that SAXPY enjoys the full $\approx6\times$ SIMD speedup. We predict instead that the same
  \emph{vectorized} kernel shows almost no speedup on a DRAM-resident array, because memory bandwidth,
  not vector throughput, then sets the pace.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{knobs}
  Case        & memory-bound SAXPY: a loop that provably vectorizes (\code{vfmadd213ps} on
                \code{ymm}, \cref{sec:vectorization}) but whose working set far exceeds L3 \\
  Build       & \code{native} (\code{-O3 -march=native}) vs \code{scalar} \\
  Script      & \code{SIMD_Advantage_Profiling/scripts/run.sh negative} \\
\end{knobs}

\subsection{Evidence}
The SAXPY loop at \code{kernels.c:25} is confirmed vectorized at \qty{256}{bit} with FMA by both the
compiler report and the disassembly in \cref{sec:vectorization}. Yet its speedup over scalar falls
monotonically with size:
\begin{center}\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    $N$ & working set & native GFLOP/s & speedup \\
    \midrule
    \num{1024}      & \qty{8}{\kibi\byte} (L1)  & 36.0 & $5.83\times$ \\
    \num{16384}     & \qty{128}{\kibi\byte} (L2) & 25.1 & $4.22\times$ \\
    \num{262144}    & \qty{2}{\mebi\byte} (L3)   & 14.9 & $2.48\times$ \\
    \num{4194304}   & \qty{32}{\mebi\byte} (DRAM) & 7.9 & $1.75\times$ \\
    \num{16777216}  & \qty{128}{\mebi\byte} (DRAM) & 5.7 & $1.41\times$ \\
    \num{33554432}  & \qty{256}{\mebi\byte} (DRAM) & 5.3 & $1.34\times$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Explanation}
The expected $8\times$ (or even the $\approx6\times$ seen in L1) does not appear in DRAM because the
kernel's arithmetic intensity (\qty{0.125}{\flop\per\byte}) places it deep in the bandwidth-bound
region of the roofline (\cref{sec:roofline}): both builds issue the same stream of \qty{64}{\byte}
cache-line requests, and the vector unit spends most of its time waiting for operands that the single
memory channel cannot deliver any faster. We verified the explanation three ways: (i) the loop is
genuinely vectorized (disassembly), so this is not a failure to use SIMD; (ii) the native DRAM
throughput (\qty{5.3}{\giga\flop\per\second}) matches the independently derived bandwidth ceiling
(\cref{sec:roofline}); and (iii) the same kernel \emph{does} hit $5.8\times$ when the array fits in
L1, so the hardware is capable of the speedup---only bandwidth withholds it.

A second, subtler misleading case appears in the dot product (\cref{sec:vectorization}): its loop
reports ``vectorized'' and the disassembly shows a \qty{256}{bit} \code{vmulps}, so a reader who
stops at the compiler report would claim success---yet the serial, non-reassociated reduction leaves
the real speedup at $\approx1\times$. Both cases carry the same lesson for AI-assisted benchmarking:
``it compiled to SIMD'' is necessary but not sufficient evidence that a kernel is actually faster.
