\section{Negative Example}\label{sec:negative}
\rubric{AI-use transparency and failure case}{10}{A meaningful negative or misleading example, verified
and explained (with \cref{sec:ai-use}).}

\begin{hypothesis}
  A naive benchmark---or a quickly generated one that only ever tests a small array---would conclude
  that SAXPY enjoys the full $\approx6\times$ SIMD speedup. We predict instead that the same
  \emph{vectorized} kernel shows almost no speedup on a DRAM-resident array, because memory bandwidth,
  not vector throughput, then sets the pace.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{knobs}
  Case        & memory-bound SAXPY: a loop that provably vectorizes (\code{vfmadd213ps} on
                \code{ymm}, \cref{sec:vectorization}) but whose working set far exceeds L3 \\
  Build       & \code{native} (\code{-O3 -march=native}) vs \code{scalar} \\
  Script      & \code{SIMD_Advantage_Profiling/scripts/run.sh negative} \\
\end{knobs}

\subsection{Evidence}
The SAXPY loop at \code{kernels.c:29} is confirmed vectorized at \qty{256}{bit} with FMA by both the
compiler report and the disassembly in \cref{sec:vectorization}. Yet its speedup over scalar falls
with size:
\begin{center}\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    $N$ & working set & native GFLOP/s & \% of roof & speedup \\
    \midrule
    \num{1024}      & \qty{8}{\kibi\byte} (L1)    & 20.1 & 54  & $5.60\times$ \\
    \num{16384}     & \qty{128}{\kibi\byte} (L2)  & 13.7 & 84  & $4.11\times$ \\
    \num{262144}    & \qty{2}{\mebi\byte} (L3)    & 8.6  & 99  & $2.82\times$ \\
    \num{4194304}   & \qty{32}{\mebi\byte} (DRAM) & 6.0  & --  & $2.31\times$ \\
    \num{8388608}   & \qty{64}{\mebi\byte} (DRAM) & 5.2  & 120 & $2.19\times$ \\
    \num{16777216}  & \qty{128}{\mebi\byte} (DRAM) & 4.7 & 109 & $1.95\times$ \\
    \num{33554432}  & \qty{256}{\mebi\byte} (DRAM) & 4.5 & 104 & $1.92\times$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
``\% of roof'' is native GFLOP/s over $B_\ell \cdot I$ with the measured triad roof of the level the
data lives in (\cref{sec:roofline}); at \qty{32}{\mebi\byte} the working set straddles L3 and DRAM.
The three SAXPY sweeps (baseline, locality, this one) are separate runs. At
$N=\num{16777216}$ they give $1.94\times$, $1.94\times$ and $1.95\times$, and at
$N=\num{33554432}$ $1.89\times$, $1.88\times$ and $1.92\times$.

\subsection{Explanation}
The hypothesis was half right. The $\approx6\times$ seen in L1 indeed does not survive in DRAM, but
the speedup does not fall to ``almost none''. It settles at $1.92\text{--}1.95\times$ in this sweep. Both parts have
measured causes. (i) The loop is genuinely vectorized (disassembly), so this is not a failure to
use SIMD. (ii) The kernel's arithmetic intensity (\qty{0.167}{\flop\per\byte},
\cref{tab:roofline-inputs}) puts it deep in the bandwidth-bound region, and native SAXPY runs at
\SIrange{104}{120}{\percent} of the DRAM roof $B_{\text{DRAM}}\cdot I = \qty{4.35}{\giga\flop\per\second}$.
$B_{\text{DRAM}}$ is measured by a separate triad benchmark, not by SAXPY. The LLC-miss counter
gives \numrange{0.122}{0.128} misses per element for native, the compulsory $2/16$
(\cref{sec:locality}). The vector loop is waiting on memory and moving no more data than it must.
\emph{No} wider vector, AVX-512 included, could make it faster. (iii) The remaining
$\approx1.9\times$ is not a SIMD gain in the memory-bound sense: scalar SAXPY is still
largely instruction-bound in DRAM (\num{2.34}~cycles per element at IPC \num{3.0}, versus
\num{1.53} with all data in L1), and the ratio of its cycles to native's (\num{1.25}) is the
measured speedup. That
ratio depends on the clock. With turbo enabled the same experiment gave only $1.34\text{--}1.41\times$
(\cref{sec:anomalies}). (iv) The same kernel \emph{does} reach $5.6\times$ when the array fits in
L1, so the hardware is capable of the speedup; in DRAM only bandwidth withholds it.

A second, subtler misleading case appears in the dot product (\cref{sec:vectorization}): its loop
reports ``vectorized'' and the disassembly shows a \qty{256}{bit} \code{vmulps}, so a reader who
stops at the compiler report would claim success---yet the serial, non-reassociated reduction leaves
the real speedup at $\approx1\times$. Both cases carry the same lesson for AI-assisted benchmarking:
``it compiled to SIMD'' is necessary but not sufficient evidence that a kernel is actually faster.
