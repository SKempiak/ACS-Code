\section{Locality Sweep}\label{sec:locality}
\rubric{Locality and memory-bound analysis}{20}{Working-set transitions identified, and the compression
of SIMD gains in the memory-bound regime explained.}

\begin{hypothesis}
  For float32 SAXPY (two arrays, \qty{8}{\byte}/element) the per-element time should be flat and low
  while the working set fits in L1 ($2N\cdot\qty{4}{\byte}\le\qty{32}{\kibi\byte}$, i.e.\
  $N\lesssim\num{4096}$), step up at the L2 boundary ($N\approx\num{32768}$) and again past L3
  ($N\approx\num{2.6e6}$), and saturate in DRAM. The scalar and SIMD curves should be far apart in
  L1 and converge once DRAM bandwidth, which both share, dominates.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{knobs}
  Fixed       & kernel SAXPY; float32; unit stride; aligned; pinned to CPU~2 \\
  Varied      & $N$ (dense grid, powers of two and midpoints, across L1/L2/LLC/DRAM); variant \\
  Counters    & user-space cycles, instructions, LLC misses and L1D read misses per repetition via
                \code{perf_event_open} (\cref{sec:ceilings-method}); written to
                \code{locality_counters.csv} when the counters can be opened \\
  Bandwidth   & effective SAXPY bandwidth $=\text{elements/s}\times\qty{12}{\byte}$ (read $x$, read and
                write $y$), compared with the independently measured triad roof of each level \\
  Script      & \code{SIMD_Advantage_Profiling/scripts/run.sh locality} \\
\end{knobs}

\subsection{Analysis}
The measured transitions line up with the cache sizes. While $N\lesssim\num{4096}$ (L1-resident)
native SAXPY takes \qtyrange{0.080}{0.100}{\nano\second} per element, a $5.6\text{--}6.9\times$
speedup over scalar's \qtyrange{0.55}{0.57}{\nano\second}. Past the \qty{32}{\kibi\byte} L1, at
$N=\num{6144}$, the native time jumps to \qty{0.144}{\nano\second} and the speedup drops to
$4.2\times$; it stays at $4.0\text{--}4.2\times$ through L2. Past the \qty{256}{\kibi\byte} L2,
at $N=\num{49152}$, it falls to $3.1\times$ (\qty{0.21}{\nano\second}), and the L3 plateau holds
$2.8\times$ (\qty{0.23}{\nano\second}) from $N=\num{98304}$ to \num{1572864}. Past L3 the gap
narrows but does \emph{not} close, contradicting the hypothesis: from $N=\num{16777216}$ on, native
settles at \qtyrange{0.43}{0.45}{\nano\second} per element and scalar at
\qtyrange{0.83}{0.85}{\nano\second}, a remaining speedup of $1.88\text{--}1.96\times$.

\Cref{tab:locality-bw,fig:locality-bw} convert the same data to bandwidth. Native SAXPY moves
\qty{118}{\gibi\byte\per\second} in L1, \num{77.8} in L2, \num{48.4} in L3 and
\qty{25.0}{\gibi\byte\per\second} from DRAM (at the representative $N$ of the table), and each level
boundary is a step in \cref{fig:locality}. The independently measured triad roofs are \num{155.6},
\num{90.5}, \num{48.6} and \qty{24.3}{\gibi\byte\per\second}, so native SAXPY reaches 76, 86, 100
and \SI{103}{\percent} of the roof. It is therefore bandwidth-bound in L3 and DRAM, where it runs at
the measured roof. In DRAM it slightly exceeds the triad roof, staying below the copy stream's
\qty{25.7}{\gibi\byte\per\second}; copy has the same two-reads-one-write pattern. In L1 and L2 it falls short: there the limit is the core's
load/store issue rate (below), not the cache. Scalar SAXPY, by contrast, is flat at
\qtyrange{17}{20.5}{\gibi\byte\per\second} through L1, L2 and L3, far below every cache roof, and
drops to \qty{13.3}{\gibi\byte\per\second} in DRAM.

\emph{Counters} (\cref{fig:locality-counters}; effective clock \qtyrange{2.78}{2.79}{\giga\hertz}
at every point). LLC misses per element stay below $10^{-5}$ up to an \qty{8}{\mebi\byte}
working set ($N=\num{1048576}$). At \num{12} and \qty{16}{\mebi\byte}, still inside the
\qty{20}{\mebi\byte} L3, they already rise to $\approx6\times10^{-4}$ and
\numrange{3.6e-3}{4.9e-3}, plausibly because the shared, not perfectly LRU L3 already loses
some lines of a working set close to its capacity. For DRAM-resident native SAXPY they reach \numrange{0.122}{0.128}
($N=\num{16777216}\ldots\num{50331648}$), matching the compulsory $2/16 = 0.125$ (two
\qty{64}{\byte} lines, $x$ and $y$, per \num{16} elements; the dirty $y$ line is written back, not
missed again). So the vector loop moves no more data than it must. Scalar shows
\numrange{0.156}{0.164}, about \SI{28}{\percent} more. Its cause is open. Untested hypothesis: at
scalar's lower request rate the L2 streamer prefetches further ahead of the demand stream, and its
prefetch requests, which the generic LLC-miss event may count, include lines evicted before use or
fetched past the end of each pass. A per-element IMC read count (see \cref{sec:anomalies}) would
show whether the extra misses are extra DRAM traffic or only extra prefetch requests. Either way, scalar does not lose because
the vector loop saves misses. The cycle counts explain the remaining gap:

\begin{center}\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    cycles/element (IPC) & L1 ($N=\num{2048}$) & L2 ($N=\num{16384}$) & L3 ($N=\num{524288}$) & DRAM ($N=\num{33554432}$) \\
    \midrule
    scalar & 1.53 (4.6) & 1.68 (4.2) & 1.81 (3.9) & 2.34 (3.0) \\
    native & 0.26 (3.1) & 0.40 (1.9) & 0.64 (1.2) & 1.25 (0.6) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

Native SAXPY's cycles per element grow $4.7\times$ from L1 to DRAM and its IPC falls to
\num{0.6}: it is waiting on memory. Scalar SAXPY already spends \num{1.53} cycles per element of
pure core work in L1, at an IPC of \num{4.6}. That is at this core's issue limit (4 fused micro-ops
per cycle, with compare-and-branch pairs macro-fused). In DRAM it needs \SI{53}{\percent} more
(\num{2.34} cycles) but still retires \num{3.0} instructions per cycle. So most of its DRAM time is
instruction execution, not waiting, and $2.34/1.25 = 1.88$ is exactly the measured DRAM speedup at
this size. This is the mechanism of the ``compression'' the rubric asks about. SIMD does not get
slower in DRAM; once native SAXPY sits on the bandwidth roof, its advantage shrinks to the scalar
loop's (mostly instruction-bound) time divided by the memory time. That ratio depends on the clock:
at turbo ($\approx\qty{4.9}{\giga\hertz}$) the same scalar work takes less time while the memory
time stays fixed, and that sweep measured only $1.32\text{--}1.44\times$ (\cref{sec:method}). Absolute native time grows step by step while scalar time is nearly flat,
which is why the \emph{ratio} shrinks at every level boundary.
