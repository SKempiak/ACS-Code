\section{Anomalies and Limitations}\label{sec:anomalies}
\rubric{Report quality}{10}{Clear plots, concise writing, honest anomalies and thoughtful limitations.}

\subsection{Unexpected results}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dot product never speeds up.} We expected a reduction to vectorize at $\approx8\times$
        like the other float32 kernels. Instead native dot stays at $\approx1\times$ across all sizes.
        Hypothesis: without \code{-ffast-math} the compiler may not reassociate floating-point
        addition, so it vectorizes the multiply but keeps a serial scalar sum. Tested: the
        disassembly shows \code{vmulps} on \code{ymm} followed by a chain of scalar \code{vaddss}
        (\cref{sec:vectorization}), confirming the hypothesis. We chose not to enable
        \code{-ffast-math} so that all variants remain bit-reproducible against the reference.
  \item \textbf{Native SAXPY speedup peaks below $N=\num{4096}$.} SAXPY reaches $6.25\times$ at
        $N=512$ in the baseline and $6.9\times$ at $N=\num{1536}$ in the locality sweep, against
        $5.75\times$ at $N=\num{4096}$. We first attributed this to per-call overhead, but the tail
        study (\cref{sec:alignment}) shows that fixed overhead \emph{lowers} the native speedup at
        small $N$. The counters point elsewhere: at $N=\num{4096}$ the two arrays fill the
        \qty{32}{\kibi\byte} L1 exactly, and L1D misses first appear there (\num{0.014} per element
        native, \num{0.025} scalar, versus at most \num{0.0002} below). The speedup peak sits at the
        largest $N$ that is still fully L1-resident.
  \item \textbf{Stencil keeps a $2.7\times$ advantage in DRAM} while SAXPY and multiply fall to
        $1.7\text{--}1.9\times$ (SAXPY) and $1.4\text{--}1.5\times$ (multiply). Its native build is just as bandwidth-bound as theirs: it runs at
        \SI{107}{\percent} of the DRAM roof (\cref{sec:roofline}). Its scalar build, however, needs
        \num{5}~FLOP per element of serial \code{mulss}/\code{addss} work and reaches only
        \qtyrange{4.0}{5.0}{\giga\flop\per\second} at every size (\qty{4.2}{\giga\flop\per\second},
        \SI{39}{\percent} of the roof, in DRAM). The retained
        ratio is again scalar's instruction cost, as for SAXPY (\cref{sec:locality}), just larger.
  \item \textbf{Cache-resident stencil is slower than L3-resident stencil.} Native stencil delivers
        \qtyrange{16.4}{17.3}{\giga\flop\per\second} for $N=\num{1024}\ldots\num{16384}$ but
        \qtyrange{19.8}{21.0}{\giga\flop\per\second} for $N=\num{65536}\ldots\num{1048576}$, and
        \num{31.4} and \qty{25.5}{\giga\flop\per\second} at the isolated sizes $N=512$ and
        \num{32768}; scalar stencil stays at \qtyrange{4.0}{5.0}{\giga\flop\per\second}. A cache
        effect would make the rate fall monotonically with $N$; this does not. Hypothesis (untested): 4K aliasing. The small \code{x} and \code{y}
        allocations sit at addresses whose difference makes a store to \code{y[i]} look like it
        conflicts with the load of \code{x[i+1]} in the low 12 address bits, which stalls the load;
        large allocations come from fresh pages with a different relative offset. Testing it would
        need a sweep over the \code{x}--\code{y} distance, which we did not run.
  \item \textbf{Scalar and SIMD SAXPY do not converge in DRAM.} We expected both to reach the same
        bandwidth plateau; native stays $1.7\text{--}1.9\times$ ahead. Our first hypothesis was that
        the scalar loop keeps fewer misses in flight. The counters (\cref{sec:locality}) show
        instead that scalar SAXPY is still \emph{instruction}-bound in DRAM (\num{1.99}~cycles per
        element at an IPC of \num{3.5}, versus \num{1.53} in L1), while native sits at the bandwidth
        roof (IPC \num{0.6}). The ratio of the two, $1.67$, matches the measured speedup, and it
        depends on the clock: the earlier turbo-clock sweep measured only $1.3\text{--}1.4\times$.
  \item \textbf{Two-state scalar SAXPY.} With L2/L3-resident data, scalar SAXPY runs at either
        $\approx1.6$ or $\approx1.8$ cycles per element (\qtyrange{0.57}{0.65}{\nano\second} at
        \qty{2.8}{\giga\hertz}). The state is chosen per process, independent of the code path
        (\cref{sec:stride}), and shows up as the alternating scalar points of
        \cref{fig:locality}, and possibly also as the scalar DRAM alignment scatter. Candidate causes are physical
        page placement or a front-end/loop-stream-detector effect. We could not separate them
        without root-only tools, so cache-resident scalar baselines carry about $\pm$\SI{6}{\percent}
        of process-to-process uncertainty, which propagates into the speedups.
  \item \textbf{Hardware gather loses to scalar.} The explicit \code{vgatherdps} kernel takes
        \qty{3.20}{\nano\second} per element at $N=\num{32768}$ versus \qty{1.92}{} (native scalar
        loop) and \qty{2.08}{\nano\second} (scalar build), i.e.\ $1.5\text{--}1.7\times$
        \emph{slower}, and it is \SI{3}{\percent} slower in DRAM. Hypothesis: the Gather Data
        Sampling microcode mitigation, which this CPU runs
        (\code{/sys/devices/system/cpu/vulnerabilities/gather_data_sampling}: \enquote{Mitigation:
        Microcode}) and which is known to make \code{vgatherdps} much slower, plus the eight scalar
        stores that AVX2 cannot vectorize. Confirming it would need a boot without the mitigation,
        which requires root.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Limited hardware counters.} We have no root, so we count only \emph{user-space} events of our own process through \code{perf_event_open}
        (\cref{sec:ceilings-method}): cycles, instructions, generic LLC misses and L1D read misses.
        Kernel-mode work, uncore/DRAM traffic counters and TLB events are not available, and the
        generic LLC-miss event's treatment of hardware-prefetch requests is model-specific, so we use
        misses per element as a relative measure between builds rather than as exact DRAM traffic.
  \item \textbf{Fixed clock.} All reported experiments were run on 2026-10-21 with governor
        \sysGovernor{}, turbo \sysTurbo{} and SMT off; the cycle counter confirms
        \qtyrange{2.78}{2.79}{\giga\hertz} for every locality and ceilings configuration but one
        (\cref{sec:method}). The fixed clock makes the results reproducible, but it is not how the
        CPU normally runs. With turbo (up to \qty{5.2}{\giga\hertz}) the compute-bound numbers (peak
        FLOP rate, cache-resident kernels, scalar builds, the dot product) scale up by roughly the
        clock ratio while DRAM bandwidth does not. So the DRAM-resident speedups reported here
        ($1.7\text{--}1.9\times$ for SAXPY) are larger than a turbo-clocked run gives ($\approx1.4\times$
        in our earlier sweep).
  \item \textbf{One machine, one compiler.} All results are from a single i9-10900F with GCC
        \sysGCC{}. Clang was used only to confirm the build compiles. No AVX-512 part was available,
        so the \qty{512}{bit} regime is out of scope.
  \item \textbf{THP always-on; no core isolation.} Transparent huge pages are \code{always}, and
        the pinned core is not isolated from the scheduler (no \code{isolcpus}). Neither is controlled
        for. The main session ran on an otherwise idle machine. During the alignment re-run
        (19:40) background work was active: a kernel worker at $\approx\SI{12}{\percent}$ of
        CPU~0, \code{udev} workers, and a web browser whose threads the scheduler could place on
        CPU~2. We kept CPU~2 for consistency with the other experiments. The cache-resident
        alignment medians reproduce the first pass to within \SI{2}{\percent}, but several
        configurations have upper-quartile outliers of up to \SI{14}{\percent} (visible as one-sided
        error bars). The DRAM-resident results are noisier than in the first pass
        (\cref{sec:alignment}).
  \item \textbf{Report build backend.} The build host has \code{bibtex} but not \code{biber}, so
        \code{biblatex} uses \code{backend=bibtex}. With that backend, the \code{ieee} style's
        citation-order sorting made every run request another BibTeX pass (``There were undefined
        references'', although the citation resolved). This report therefore sorts its single
        reference by name/year, and it compiles in final mode without that warning.
\end{itemize}
